2. Speed-to-lead: the cheapest edge you have

Harvard Business Review audited 2,241 US companies. Firms that replied to a web lead within an hour were nearly 7× as likely to have a meaningful conversation with a decision-maker as firms that waited even one hour longer. The average response time was 42 hours, and 23% never replied at all. Agencies with account managers are slow. A solo engineer who answers in 20 minutes looks senior and organised.

  • Inbound or referral: reply within 1 working hour. A short holding reply counts: "Thanks, I've read it. Two quick questions so I don't waste your time: … Here's my calendar if a 30-min call is easier: {{CAL_LINK}}".
  • Indian clients: if they started on WhatsApp, reply on WhatsApp. A 30-second voice note is fine. Move the details to email afterwards so there's a paper trail.
  • HN and job-post leads: same day. The first 10 replies get read, the 60th doesn't. That's why scripts/hn-freelance.mjs exists.
  • Marketplaces: send proposals only on posts under ~2 hours old (Lead Gen → Marketplaces).
  • Turn on phone notifications for your business email and LinkedIn messages, but only during work blocks.
  • Create a free scheduling link (Cal.com or Calendly) with 30-min "Discovery" and 15-min "Quick question" slots and a 2-hour minimum notice.
  • Save the holding reply above as a text snippet on your laptop and phone.

Instant disqualifiers

Asks for free spec work or an unpaid "test task" larger than about 1 hour. Wants payment via crypto or to "overpay and refund". Wants to move off Upwork before a contract. Won't put anything in writing. Wants you to run their repo on your machine "quickly" (see Risks). Budget is clearly a tenth of the work.

Yellow flags (price them in, or decline)

"It's a simple app, should take a weekend." Third developer on the project. Five decision-makers. Wants daily calls. Pushes for hourly with a cap. Changes scope during the first call. Every one of these needs a tighter scope, a bigger buffer and stricter payment terms.

Green flags

Names a budget range. Has a deadline tied to money (sale, launch, funding). Shares data (orders/month, AOV, RTO%). Has hired freelancers before and paid on time. Introduced by someone you trust. Asks about your process, not just your rate.

4. The 30-minute discovery call

The goal is not to "pitch". It's to find out whether there's a valuable problem you can solve, what it's worth, and what the next step is. You should talk less than 40% of the time. Full script with per-offer questions: discovery-call-script.md.

  1. 0–2 min, set the frame. "Thanks for making time. My plan: I'll ask a few questions to understand what's going on, then tell you honestly whether I'm a fit and what I'd suggest as a next step. If I'm not the right person, I'll say so. Sound good?"
  2. 2–12 min, the situation and the pain. "What's prompting this now?" · "Walk me through how it works today." · "What have you tried?" · "What does it cost you, in orders, hours or risk?"
  3. 12–18 min, the outcome. "If we're talking in 3 months and this went really well, what changed?" · "How will you measure it?" Write their words down. They go into the proposal.
  4. 18–23 min, constraints. Timeline, who decides, budget range (wording below), tech access, other vendors.
  5. 23–28 min, reflect and suggest a next step. Summarise in their words. "Here's what I'd suggest as a first step…" (usually a paid audit/sprint, §6). Name the price range.
  6. 28–30 min, lock it in. "I'll send three options by tomorrow 6 pm. Can we take 15 minutes on Thursday to walk through them?" Put that meeting in the calendar before you hang up.

5. Diagnose, then prescribe (and don't consult for free)

Doctors don't hand out prescriptions in the waiting room. Engineers in sales mode often do the opposite: they start solving on the call ("Oh, you just need to move that to a webhook queue and…"). The client walks away with your best idea for free.

  • On the call: name the category of fix, not the steps. "This looks like a third-party script problem plus an unoptimised image pipeline. Both are fixable without a redesign."
  • Show you know the territory: one short story, not a lecture. "At Cultus, partial COD for high-risk pincodes was the lever for us. The rules matter more than the gateway."
  • Then prescribe the next step: a paid audit or a fixed sprint, with a price range.
  • If they push for specifics: "Happy to go deep. That's exactly what the audit is for. It's credited if you go ahead with the sprint."

6. Sell a small paid first step

With no portfolio and no reviews, the hardest sale is a ₹4L build. The easiest is a small, fixed, low-risk first step that proves you're good. Then you expand. This is "paid discovery" (also called roadmapping): the client pays you to diagnose, you deliver a written plan, and the plan becomes the proposal for the real project.

7. Proposals that win

A proposal confirms what you already agreed on the call. It is not where you persuade them. If anything in it surprises the client, the call didn't do its job. Build it in the Proposal Builder (prints to PDF) from your offer presets.

Structure (1–2 pages)

  1. Situation: their problem in their words, from your call notes.
  2. Outcome and how we'll measure it: "Mobile LCP under 2.5s on product pages; conversion tracked weekly for 4 weeks."
  3. Three options: Essential / Recommended / Premium, at a price ratio of about 1 : 1.6 : 2.5. Highlight the middle one. Each option is a clear list of deliverables plus a timeline.
  4. Why me: 2–3 lines of relevant proof (Cultus metrics, Deribit, Twinntax), plus one link.
  5. Timeline: start date (after deposit), milestones, the client's responsibilities (access, feedback within 2 working days).
  6. Assumptions and out of scope: this is where scope creep is stopped before it starts.
  7. Investment and terms: 50% to start / 50% on delivery (40/40/20 above ₹2L or $5k), Net 7, prices exclude GST where applicable, IP transfers on full payment (Payment terms).
  8. Valid for 14 days. Then a single next step: "Reply 'Recommended' and I'll send the agreement and deposit invoice the same day."

Value framing: price against their numbers, not your hours

Example (A1, Indian D2C):
  12,000 sessions/month on mobile × 1.2% conversion × ₹1,400 AOV = ₹2.0L/month revenue from mobile
  +0.2 pts conversion after a speed sprint  → +₹33,600/month
  Recommended option ₹48,000 → pays back in ~6 weeks, and keeps paying.
Use their real numbers from the call. Never promise the lift. Show the arithmetic and let them judge.

Send it, then walk them through it

  • Send within 24 hours of the call, with a short email (proposal-email.md).
  • Walk-through call (15 min): share your screen and go through it top to bottom. Stop after the options: "Which of these feels closest?" Then be quiet.
  • Never send the PDF and wait. Unpresented proposals get forwarded, compared on price alone, and forgotten.

10. Negotiation: trade, don't discount

  • Price is the last thing that moves. First move scope (drop to the Essential tier), then time (a slower timeline), then terms (100% upfront for 5% off), then proof (a named case study, testimonial or referral call). Never give a discount without getting something back. See the full list in Pricing → Discounts.
  • Founding-client pricing (first 3 clients only): 25–30% off, in writing, with an expiry date, in exchange for a case study, a testimonial and a referral call. It's a trade, not charity.
  • Indian D2C clients will usually counter. Don't pad your prices to leave room. Sell fixed packages and answer a counter with scope: "To get to ₹35k, we'd skip the app/script cleanup. You keep the image and theme fixes." Your hourly floor is ₹1,200. Below it, walk away.
  • Know your walk-away point before the call. Your alternative is your pipeline. That's why §1 matters: a full pipeline makes you a calm negotiator.
  • Use silence and calibrated questions (Chris Voss): "How am I supposed to do that for half?" · "What would need to be true for the Recommended option to work?"
  • Don't negotiate against yourself. If they say "too high" without a number, ask: "What did you have in mind?"
  • Put every change in writing the same day (scope, price, dates), so the agreement matches what was said.

Playbook with give/get table and scripts: negotiation-playbook.md.

11. Closing: contract + deposit = start

  1. Verbal yes → "Great. I'll send the agreement and the deposit invoice within the hour."
  2. Agreement: under about ₹1L, use the one-page agreement. Above that, use the MSA (India / international) plus an SOW. Electronic signatures are valid under India's IT Act 2000. Stamp-duty and other details are in Contracts.
  3. Deposit invoice: generated in the Invoice Generator (GST, non-GST or export-under-LUT version). Payment by UPI/NEFT/Razorpay link for India, or your payment rail for abroad.
  4. Deposit cleared → schedule the kickoff, send the onboarding questionnaire and the access checklist (Delivery Ops).
  5. Update the CRM: stage won, value, and the start date. Book a "testimonial ask" reminder for delivery day.
No deposit, no start. Not even "just setting up the repo". Work before payment teaches the client that your terms don't matter. It also leaves you with no leverage if they disappear.

12. Marketplace sales (Upwork, Contra, talent networks)

  • Proposals: 120–180 words. The proof goes in the first two lines (those are all the client sees in the list). One sharp question. No "Dear Sir/Madam", no CV dump. Full playbook: Lead Gen → Marketplaces; templates: upwork-and-marketplaces.md.
  • Interview = discovery call. Same script, same 3-option follow-up (as fixed-price milestones).
  • Escrow is your deposit. Don't start until the milestone is funded, and never accept "I'll fund it after you start".
  • Stay on the platform. Upwork clients must stay on Upwork for 24 months unless the conversion fee is paid. Asking to move off-platform can get your account suspended. Off-platform requests are also the most common scam opener (Risks).
  • Fees: Upwork's fee is 0–15% per contract, plus 18% GST on the fee for India-based freelancers. Gross up your quotes (Pricing → Gross-up).
  • Talent networks (Toptal, Arc, Flexiple, Braintrust): the network sells. Your job is to pass the vetting and then run a great client interview with the same discovery questions.
