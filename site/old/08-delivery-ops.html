Bottom line. Clients can't judge your code, so they judge how the project feels. Run every project on the same rails: no work before the deposit, a structured kickoff, a written update every Friday, a demo every 1–2 weeks, change requests before any extra work, tests on every money path, a 5-business-day acceptance window, a clean handover where the client owns every account, and a 30-day warranty. Then turn the happy ending into four assets: a testimonial, a case study, a referral and a care plan. Your Cultus habits (115 test files on money paths, 17 ADRs) are a delivery standard most freelancers can't match, so sell them. Never carry more than two active builds, and keep about 20% of your week for sales even when you're busy. Dropping sales when work is plentiful is what causes the feast-and-famine cycle.

Client-owned everything

The repo lives in the client's GitHub org from day 1. Hosting, database, Shopify app, payment gateway, domain and SaaS are all in their name and on their card, and you're an invited member with least privilege. This makes handover trivial, protects you from becoming their unpaid bank, and makes firing (either way) clean. Platform specifics:

  • Shopify: send a collaborator request from your Partner/Dev Dashboard. The owner may need to give you a 4-digit request code and chooses your permissions. Build on a development store or a duplicate theme first.
  • Vercel: the Hobby plan is non-commercial personal use only, so a client's production store or SaaS needs Pro/Enterprise. Raise this at onboarding, as it's a real cost line ("Third-party costs" in the SOW).
  • Supabase: the Developer role by default. It gives content access but can't change project settings, so ask for Administrator only temporarily.
  • Razorpay/Stripe: team-member access, test mode first. The owner creates live keys and webhook secrets.
"If the code is in their repo from day 1, how does 'IP on full payment' protect me?" Legally, their licence is conditional and revocable until they pay (MSA cl. 9.4). Practically, your protection is the deposit + milestone billing + stop-work clause, plus the fact that production go-live and launch support come after the final payment. For unknown clients, keep the final milestone on a branch in your fork and merge it when the last invoice clears. Say so at kickoff and nobody is surprised.

Communication rules

  1. One channel of record. Decisions live in email or the shared channel. Calls and WhatsApp produce a one-line written confirmation: "Confirming: we go with option B."
  2. Friday update, every week, no exceptions. Status colour, done, next, needed from you with dates, decisions, scope/budget, risks (template). It takes 10 minutes and prevents most escalations.
  3. Demo every 1–2 weeks on a real preview link, or as a 3-minute Loom.
  4. Bad news within 24 hours, with a plan and options. Clients forgive delays. They don't forgive surprises.
  5. Response times are written down: 1 business day normally; 4 business hours for production issues; out-of-hours emergencies at 1.5× or under a Priority care plan.
  6. Two inbox windows a day (e.g. 11:30 and 17:30 IST) protect deep-work blocks. Clients adapt quickly when you're predictable.
  7. Every meeting has an agenda beforehand and a recap afterwards. No recap means it didn't happen.

Quality bar: your Definition of Done

Nothing is "done" until all of these are true. Put this list in proposals: it's what separates a product engineer from a cheap freelancer.

  • Self-reviewed PR with a description and a screenshot or preview link; small PRs (< 400 lines where possible)
  • Tests on every money path: pricing, discounts and coupons, checkout, payment webhooks (signature verification + idempotency), refunds, inventory sync, COD rules
  • CI green (lint, type-check, tests) on GitHub Actions; no merges on red
  • An ADR for each significant decision (why X over Y, alternatives, consequences), one page max
  • Preview deploy reviewed; Shopify theme changes previewed on a duplicate theme
  • Errors tracked (Sentry or similar) and logs free of secrets and personal data
  • Performance budget checked on key pages (Lighthouse mobile); no regression vs baseline
  • Accessibility basics: semantic HTML, labels, keyboard focus, contrast
  • Security: Supabase RLS policies with a test that proves cross-tenant reads fail; secrets only in env vars; dependencies audited
  • README and runbook updated in the same PR when behaviour changes
Sell the habit. "On my own marketplace I wrote 115 test files covering pricing, refunds, coupons and Shopify sync, and 17 architecture decision records. You'll get the same discipline: a codebase another engineer can take over in a day." Put this in your proposal's "How I work" section and on your site. It answers the buyer's biggest fear about freelancers: that they'll be stuck with an unmaintainable codebase.

Change control without friction

Most scope creep begins with you saying "sure" on a call. Use the same four moves every time: Acknowledge → Classify → Offer 3 options → Log.

  • Classify: a defect against the acceptance criteria is free (warranty). Something new is a change request. Something tiny (under 30 minutes) can come from the goodwill budget.
  • Three options: A) add now (cost + new date), B) swap for an existing scope item, C) defer to phase 2 or the care plan. Clients pick a trade-off, and nobody has to hear "no".
  • Goodwill budget: about 1 hour a week of tiny tweaks you absorb, logged as ₹0 CRs so the client sees the value and it doesn't become the norm.
  • Never work on unapproved changes, especially over a weekend before a launch.

Scripts for the awkward moments ("this should take 5 minutes", "our CEO wants changes after sign-off"): scope-creep-responses.md.

Acceptance, handover and warranty

  1. Ready for review: send the preview link, a short test script (5–10 steps that mirror the acceptance criteria) and the review deadline (5 business days).
  2. Punch list: the client lists specific, reproducible failures against the criteria. You fix them once and re-submit only those parts. Anything else is a change request.
  3. Deemed acceptance: silence after the window, or production use, counts as acceptance. Say so politely in the "ready for review" email.
  4. Final invoice → payment → handover: production go-live, repo tag, docs, account ownership check, secret rotation list, your access removed (checklist).
  5. Warranty (30 days): defects against acceptance criteria are free. New behaviour, third-party or platform changes, and other people's edits are not.

How to fire a client gracefully (steps + script)
  1. Reach a clean stopping point: the end of the current milestone, or a stable branch.
  2. Give notice per the contract (15 days), in writing. Be brief and kind, and don't list grievances.
  3. Offer a proper handover and, if you can, a referral to another developer.
  4. Invoice the work done; refund any unearned part of the deposit promptly.
  5. Remove your access and send the rotation list. Keep all records.

Thank you for the opportunity."

Late payments: prevention first, then escalation

Prevention beats collection. Take a deposit, cap exposure at one milestone, collect billing details and the PO process at onboarding, send invoices to both the decision-maker and accounts payable with a payment link, and go live only after the final payment. Then run the reminder ladder mechanically:

Rule of thumb. If recovery would cost more than about half the debt, write it off, record the lesson in your retro, and tighten deposits. Your time is better spent closing the next client.

Security and data hygiene

  • Least privilege, named accounts, MFA, password-manager sharing only: the credentials policy is client-facing, so attach it to proposals.
  • No production personal data on your laptop. Use seed or anonymised data. Under DPDP you're the client's data processor: instructions only, safeguards, 24-hour breach notice (contracts → DPDP).
  • AI tools under a written policy: no secrets or personal data in prompts, no-training settings, human review of every line (AI usage policy).
  • Unknown repos only in a disposable VM or container. Fake-recruiter and fake-client malware campaigns target developers through "take-home tests" and "fix this bug" repos (Risks & scams).
  • Offboarding is part of delivery: remove access, send the rotation list, delete local copies, and confirm in writing.

Capacity: how much you can carry

  • Sustainable billable load: 25–30 hours a week. The rest goes to sales, admin, learning and the weekly updates themselves.
  • Concurrency cap: at most 2 active builds plus 3–4 care plans. A third build means everything slips.
  • Estimate buffer: +20–30% on fixed-price work. Integrations (Shopify, Razorpay, legacy APIs) need the higher end.
  • Keep about 20% for pipeline even when fully booked: outreach, content, follow-ups (Metrics & cadence).
  • Seasonality: Indian D2C freezes deploys around the festive season (Oct–Nov) and end-of-season sales (around Dec–Jan and Jun–Jul). Global e-commerce freezes around Black Friday/Cyber Monday (late Nov). Sell before these (speed sprints, health checks) and schedule builds after.
