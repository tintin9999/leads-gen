Every developer gets stuck, and some jobs turn out bigger than they looked. The client can handle bad news; what hurts is hearing it late.

This page shows you how to tell the client early, with a new date and a plan.

Day 3 of a 4-day job

Maya is a made-up founder in the US. Her app "Fernleaf" was built with an AI app builder on Next.js and Supabase. She hired you on Upwork for a $300 fixed-price job: fix login and fix Stripe payments, in 4 days.

Login was fixed on day 2. On day 3 you find the Stripe bug is deeper.

The app never checks Stripe's messages to the server (webhooks). So paid orders sometimes stay "unpaid". Fixing it properly needs 2 more days.

You have two choices on day 3. You can stay silent and hope. Or you can tell her today.

Here is the message you send that afternoon:

"Hi Maya, an update on the Stripe bug. It is deeper than it looked. The app never checks Stripe's payment messages, so some paid orders stay marked unpaid.

The proper fix needs 2 more days, so delivery moves from Thursday to Saturday. I'll send a short progress note each evening.

If Thursday matters more, I can ship a smaller fix by then and do the full one as a separate job. Which do you prefer?"

Silence compared with telling early

If you stay silent, Thursday comes and nothing arrives. Maya feels ignored. She may ask for a refund or leave a low review.

If you tell her on day 3, she gets facts, a new date and a choice. Most clients say "thanks for the heads-up". Some even like you more for it.

The rule: the moment you think a date may slip, tell the client that day. Give the reason in one line, a new date, and a plan.

A short routine for being stuck

Being stuck for an hour is normal. Being stuck for two days in silence is the problem. Use a fixed routine:

  1. Set a time limit: 90 minutes on one problem without progress.
  2. When the time is up, write the problem down in five lines: what you expect, what happens, and what you tried.
  3. Make the smallest example that shows the bug, with fake data and no client code.
  4. Search the official docs and the issue list of the library, for example Stripe's or Supabase's.
  5. Ask for help in a public forum or a developer community, using only your small example.
  6. If you are still stuck after half a day, tell the client, as in the message above.

Never paste the client's code, passwords, keys or customer data into a forum or a chat group. Replace real names with fake ones, like order_123 and user@example.com. If a key leaks by mistake, change it at once and tell the client the same day.

Using AI tools within the client's rules

AI coding tools can get you unstuck fast. They are fine to use, within some rules.

  • First check the job post and chat. If the client says "no AI tools", follow that.
  • Use a plan or setting where your input is not used to train the tool. Check this in the tool's privacy settings.
  • Paste the smallest part: one function and the error message, not the whole project.
  • Never paste secrets: no .env files, keys or customer records.
  • Read and test every line the tool writes. You own the result, not the tool.

Login and payment code needs extra care. Prefer well-known libraries to code a tool made up. Your full checklist is in the AI usage policy.

Offer choices, in this order

When a date will slip, give the client choices. Start with the one that costs her least.

  1. A new date. Same work, same price, later delivery. This suits most cases.
  2. A smaller job. Deliver part on the old date. The rest becomes a new milestone, a separately funded step, or a later job.
  3. A part refund. Use this last, when you cannot deliver part of what you promised.

On a direct client job, the same order works.

Asha is a made-up owner of "Indigo Thread", a Pune clothing brand on Shopify. If her ₹48,000 Store Speed Sprint will be 3 days late, offer the new date first. Next, offer the fixed pages now and the rest by Monday.

Agree the change in writing, in Upwork chat or by email. Then keep the new date.

When to end a job you cannot do

Sometimes the job is beyond what you can do in a fair time. For example, the bug is in a service you have no access to. Ending the job honestly is better than failing slowly.

  1. Write down what you finished and what is left.
  2. Send the client your notes and any working fixes.
  3. Offer a fair refund for the part you did not do.
  4. On Upwork, agree the amount in chat, then end the contract from the contract page.
  5. Thank her and suggest the kind of expert she needs.

"Maya, I've fixed login, and here are my notes on the Stripe issue. The rest needs access to your old payment service, which we don't have.

I'd rather be honest than keep you waiting. I'll refund $180 for the Stripe part."

This is rare, and it is not the end of your career. A calm, honest exit often still earns a fair review.

Do this now

  • Save the day-3 message as a note you can copy and edit.
  • Set a 90-minute timer habit for your next hard bug.
  • Turn off training on your inputs in each AI tool you use.
  • Read the AI usage policy once.
