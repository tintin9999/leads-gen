Bottom line. A contract's job is to protect five things: cash (deposit, Net 7, stop-work), scope (a SOW with testable acceptance criteria and a change-request process), IP (the code passes to the client only on full payment, with wording that defeats the Copyright Act's 5-year/India-only defaults), risk (liability capped at the fees, no uncapped indemnities) and exit (paid kill fee, cheap dispute path). Use one Master Services Agreement per client and one short SOW per project. For jobs under about ₹50k/$1k, use a one-page agreement. In Maharashtra, stamp duty is ₹500; then e-sign. Never sign a non-compete: it's void in India, but it still causes trouble. Register on Udyam before you sign Indian contracts, because the MSMED Act is your strongest collection weapon. The five things every contract must protect

1 · Cash

Deposit before work (50%, or 40/40/20 above ₹2L/$5k; 100% for jobs ≤ ₹25k). Net 7. Late interest. Stop-work after 14 days overdue. Exposure never more than one milestone.

2 · Scope

Deliverables with pass/fail acceptance criteria, a long out-of-scope list, client dependencies with dates, 2 revision rounds, and change requests for everything else.

3 · IP

You own the code until paid. Assign on full payment, for the full term, worldwide, with no s.19(4) lapse. Keep your Background IP and license it to the client.

4 · Risk

Liability capped at fees paid under the SOW (12 months). No indirect loss. Narrow copyright-only indemnity. 30-day fix warranty. No business-outcome guarantees.

5 · Exit

Termination pays for work done plus a 25% kill fee on the current milestone. Cheap dispute path: MSME Samadhaan, fast-track arbitration or Pune courts. No non-compete.

Why the IP clause is the one freelancers in India get wrong

Under the Copyright Act, 1957, the employer owns code an employee writes (a "contract of service"). A freelancer works under a "contract for service", so the freelancer is the first owner of the code (s.17). Ownership moves to the client only through a written, signed assignment (s.18–19). Three defaults catch people out:

  • s.19(5): if the assignment doesn't state a duration, it's deemed to be 5 years.
  • s.19(6): if it doesn't state territory, it's presumed to be India only.
  • s.19(4): if the assignee doesn't exercise the rights within 1 year, the assignment lapses, unless the contract says otherwise.

The Delhi High Court applied the term and territory defaults to a software assignment in Pine Labs v Gemalto. Your templates fix all three ("full term of copyright, worldwide, shall not lapse under s.19(4)") and state the consideration. Point this out to the client's lawyer as a favour to them. It builds trust and costs you nothing, because the assignment only takes effect on full payment.

Indian law you must know (and use)

Non-competes are void; non-solicits must be narrow

Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 voids agreements in restraint of trade. Courts have consistently refused to enforce post-term non-competes (Superintendence Co. v Krishan Murgai). Non-solicitation clauses are treated more kindly if they are narrow and time-limited, but enforceability is uncertain. Your stance: strike non-competes. Offer confidentiality plus a 6-month staff non-solicit. For agencies, accept a narrow End-Client non-solicit with a release fee instead of a ban.

Late fees, kill fees and liquidated damages (s.73–74)

Indian courts award only reasonable compensation, up to any amount named in the contract (s.74; Kailash Nath v DDA, SC 2015). Your drafting choices follow from that. Frame late fees as interest. Frame the kill fee as a genuine pre-estimate of loss from reserved capacity, and keep it modest (25% of the current milestone). Apply the deposit to work done plus the kill fee rather than calling it blanket "non-refundable": a forfeiture with no link to actual loss is the weakest position in court. The same rule protects you against client-imposed liquidated damages, but don't rely on a court. Strike or cap them.

The MSMED Act: your strongest collection lever

  1. Register on Udyam first: it's free and takes about 30 minutes (setup memo). In Silpi Industries (SC 2021), MSMED remedies were denied to a supplier who registered after the contract. In NBCC v State of West Bengal (SC 2025), a three-judge bench took a broader view, and the question has gone to a larger bench. Until that's settled, register before you sign.
  2. Payment window: buyers must pay by the agreed date, which cannot exceed 45 days from acceptance. If no date was agreed, the deadline is 15 days. Silence for 15 days after delivery counts as deemed acceptance (s.15, s.2(b)). Your 5-business-day acceptance clause fits comfortably inside this window.
  3. Interest: compound, with monthly rests, at 3× the RBI bank rate (s.16). With the bank rate at 5.75% after the 7 Oct 2026 hike, that's about 17.25% a year, compounded. Courts treat it as mandatory and overriding the contract.
  4. The tax nudge: buyers can't deduct the expense until they pay you. This rule was s.43B(h) and is now s.37(2)(g) of the Income-tax Act 2025. Mention it politely in day +7 reminders (ladder).
  5. Enforcement: file on MSME Samadhaan. The state's Facilitation Council runs conciliation, then arbitration, and is meant to decide within 90 days. A buyer who wants to challenge the award must deposit 75% of it first (s.19). That deposit requirement is why buyers settle.
Scope. The MSMED Act helps only with Indian buyers, and only if you're registered as a micro or small enterprise (a freelancer usually qualifies as micro). Foreign clients are covered by your deposit, not by this Act.

DPDP Act: you are the client's data processor

The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified on 13 Nov 2025. Most obligations apply from about 13 May 2027: notices, security safeguards, breach reporting, and processor contracts. When you touch customer data (orders, phone numbers, addresses), the client is the Data Fiduciary and you are a Data Processor. Rule 6(1)(f) expects the contract between them to require reasonable security safeguards, and Rule 6(1)(e) expects logs to be kept for a year. The fiduciary must report breaches to the Board, so promise 24-hour breach notice to the client. Bigger clients are already writing these terms into vendor contracts, so accept the reasonable ones. Templates: MSA cl. 11, credentials policy.

GST and TDS clauses

Write fees exclusive of GST, adding 18% if you're registered and stating "not registered" if you aren't. Under the 2025 Act, Indian clients deduct TDS under s.393: 10% for professional services or 2% for "fees for technical services", above a ₹50,000-a-year threshold. Software work is classified both ways in practice, so ask at onboarding. Make the clause require TDS on the fee excluding GST, a certificate, and the credit showing against your PAN. Mechanics are in Tax & Money → TDS.

Keeping e-records usable as evidence

Contracts formed by email or e-signature are valid (IT Act s.10A; Trimex v Vedanta, SC 2010, where the contract was formed through emails). To use them in court, electronic records need a certificate under s.63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which replaced Evidence Act s.65B from 1 July 2024. In practice: keep the signed PDF and the e-sign audit trail, keep email threads in their original mailbox (not only forwarded copies), and never delete client Slack or WhatsApp exports from a disputed project.

  • Register on Udyam before signing your first Indian contract; put the Udyam number in the MSA, invoices and email signature
  • Make "IP passes on full payment, full term, worldwide, no s.19(4) lapse" non-negotiable in every template
  • Create a /Clients/<client>/Contracts folder convention: signed PDF + audit trail + approved CRs + key emails

Stamping and e-signing in Maharashtra

Stamp duty. A services agreement or NDA falls under the residual entry for agreements, Article 5(h)(B) of the Maharashtra Stamp Act. The duty rose from ₹100 to ₹500 on 14 Oct 2024. An unstamped agreement is not void, but courts won't admit it as evidence until the duty and a penalty are paid. The Supreme Court's 7-judge bench confirmed in Dec 2023 that this is a curable defect, and that arbitration clauses in such contracts still work. At ₹500, stamping any Indian contract worth ₹1L or more is cheap insurance.

  1. Pay the duty on or before the signing date. Options: franking at an authorised bank; a GRAS e-challan (gras.mahakosh.gov.in; keep the GRN); or digital stamp paper through e-sign platforms with Maharashtra e-stamping (SignDesk, Leegality, Zoho Sign's integration). Banks' e-SBTR often has a ₹5,000 minimum, so it rarely works for ₹500. Maharashtra's online e-stamping is being rolled out. Check what's live when you need it.
  2. Attach the stamp certificate or challan as page 1 of the agreement PDF, or let the e-stamp platform do it.
  3. E-sign through any reputable platform. Both parties sign, and you keep the final PDF plus the audit trail.
  4. Who pays? The MSA says which party; you can simply absorb ₹500. Foreign clients signing abroad: stamping only matters if you'd ever enforce the contract in India. Do it for large deals.

When the client sends their contract

Bigger clients and agencies will send their own paper. Don't swap it for yours. Redline it. Process:

  1. Read in three passes: money (payment, termination, set-off), then ownership (IP, non-compete, confidentiality), then risk (warranty, liability, indemnity, liquidated damages, insurance, data).
  2. Mark severity with the red-flag table. 🔴 must change, 🟠 should change, 🟢 concede freely.
  3. Redline 3–6 items at most. Use tracked changes with one-line margin comments. Long redlines from a solo freelancer look difficult; short, reasoned ones look professional.
  4. Send the cover note (script at the bottom of the red-flag file), and offer a 15-minute call.
  5. Know your walk-away list before the call: no deposit from an unknown client, IP vesting before payment with no deposit, uncapped liability, a non-compete in your niche, pay-when-paid with no backstop.

Platform contracts (Upwork, Contra, talent networks)

  • Upwork: the client and you contract under Upwork's terms, plus its Optional Service Contract Terms unless you agree otherwise. Those terms broadly match your stance: work product passes to the client on full payment, and disclosed background technology is licensed rather than assigned. Upwork's non-circumvention rule bars taking a client found through Upwork off-platform for 24 months unless the conversion fee is paid. Breaking it can get your account suspended. "Let's move off Upwork to save fees" is also a classic scam opener. Put scope and acceptance criteria in the contract description (paste your SOW), use milestones (escrow), and sign client NDAs through Upwork's NDA feature where offered.
  • Talent networks (Toptal and similar): the network usually contracts with the client, and you sign the network's freelancer agreement. Read its IP, payment-timing, non-solicit and exclusivity clauses closely. Your MSA doesn't apply, but your SOW habits (scope, acceptance, CRs) still do.
  • Contra and other 0%-commission platforms: they often provide contract templates. Compare them with your clause library before accepting the defaults.

Your contract SOP: from "yes" to kickoff

  1. Verbal yes: within 24 hours, send the SOW (or one-pager) built from the accepted proposal (proposal builder). First-time client: MSA + SOW together.
  2. Negotiate in one round if possible, using the clause library. Confirm in writing what changed.
  3. Stamp (Indian deals ≥ ₹1L) → e-sign → save the signed PDF + audit trail in the client folder.
  4. Invoice the deposit the same day (invoice generator), sending it to the decision-maker and accounts payable.
  5. Deposit clears → send the onboarding questionnaire and book the kickoff. No work before this point, not even "just setting up the repo".
  6. Update the CRM (CRM): stage Won, contract value, payment dates, renewal or retainer date.
