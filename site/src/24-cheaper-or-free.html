Almost every client asks for a lower price at some point. You do not need to argue or give in. Change the size of the job, not the price of your time.

Three asks and three replies

Maya is a made-up founder in the US. Her app "Fernleaf" was built with an AI app builder on Next.js and Supabase. You quoted $300, fixed price, on Upwork.

Ask one: "Can you do it for $150?"

"I can't do the same work for $150, but I can make it fit. For $150 I can fix the data rules so users only see their own orders.

The payment fix and tests can be a second milestone later. Would that work?"

Ask two: "Do a small free test first, then we'll hire you."

"I don't do unpaid work, but I understand wanting less risk. I can start with a $50 milestone: I review your Supabase rules and send the five biggest risks in one day. If you like it, we continue."

Ask three: "We can't pay much now, but we'll give you company shares."

"Thank you, but I don't take shares in place of pay. I'm one person and my bills are in rupees. If cash is tight, let's do the most urgent part now and plan the rest for later."

Each reply says no to the cut, then offers a smaller job. The client gets a choice instead of a refusal.

The rule, in numbers

Here is Maya's $300 job, split into named parts with honest hours.

Part 1: fix the data rules        3 hours   $120
Part 2: fix the Stripe payment     2.5 hours $100
Part 3: tests and a short report   2 hours   $80
Total                              7.5 hours $300
Your hourly rate: $300 ÷ 7.5 =      $40 an hour

Now Maya wants to pay $200. There are two ways to get there.

Right way: remove Part 2
Work left: 3 + 2 = 5 hours
Price: $120 + $80 = $200
Your hourly rate: $200 ÷ 5 = $40 an hour

Wrong way: same work, lower price
Work: 7.5 hours
Price: $200
Your hourly rate: $200 ÷ 7.5 = $26.67 an hour

The wrong way puts you below your floor of $35 an hour. Upwork's fee then comes off that too. The right way keeps your rate and gives her a smaller, real result.

Free test work

A free "test task" is often free labour. Sometimes the feature ships in their product and you never hear back. Upwork's own rules say requests for free work break its policies.

Offer a small paid first step instead. Good first steps for you:

  • A review of the app's login and data rules, with a written list of risks: $50–100.
  • One small fix, as the first milestone of the bigger job.
  • A one-hour paid call to plan the work, on an hourly contract.

Some tests are normal. A short coding exercise of up to an hour, on the client's own website, is fine. Running their code on your laptop is not: see A stranger asks you to run their code.

Your floor, and the first-three-jobs price

Your floor is the lowest rate you accept: $35 an hour on Upwork. Write it on a note next to your screen. Below it, you are paying to work.

Your first three Upwork jobs are already priced to win reviews: small fixed jobs of $150–500, or $35–40 an hour. That is your discount. Do not cut below it.

It is fine to say so openly. Clients like knowing why the price is good, and that it will end.

"I'm new on Upwork, so I'm taking a few jobs at $35 an hour to build reviews. My normal rate is $40 and goes up from March."

After three good reviews, the price goes to $45–50. You do not need to explain that to new clients. It is simply your rate.

When to walk away

Walk away politely when any of these is true:

  • The budget is below your floor, and they will not make the job smaller.
  • They insist on free work before any contract.
  • They keep pushing the price down after you agreed.
  • They want to pay later: "after launch", "after funding", or "when our client pays us".

"I understand. At that budget I would have to rush, and that wouldn't be fair to you. I hope you find the right person, and I'm here if the plan changes."

Losing a cheap job feels bad in the first weeks. A job below your floor is worse: it uses the hours you need for better proposals. Most low-budget clients also leave the harshest reviews.

With a direct client

A direct client is one you found outside a portal. You do not need this yet. The same rule works, in rupees.

Asha is a made-up owner of "Indigo Thread", a Pune clothing brand on Shopify. She asks for ₹30,000 off your ₹48,000 Store Speed Sprint.

Offer the smaller ₹30,000 size, with fewer pages fixed. Your India floor is ₹1,200 an hour.

Do this now

  • Write your floor, $35 an hour, on a note near your screen.
  • Split your next quote into two or three named parts with hours and prices.
  • Choose your paid first step and its price, for example a $50 review.
  • Save the three replies above as snippets you can paste.
